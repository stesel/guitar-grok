# Horror Metal 07 — Riff Hooks: Writing the Part Everyone Remembers

**Goal:** Write an 8-bar riff containing one unmistakable rhythmic hook — something a listener can tap or remember after hearing it once.

## Setup

- Guitar Pro 8
- Drop D
- 4/4 at 170 BPM
- Rhythm guitar, bass and drums
- Allowed chords: D5, F5, G5

## Rhythm Lab — 5 minutes

Set the metronome to **90 BPM** and clap:

```text
1 e & a | 2 e & a | 3 e & a | 4 e & a
X - X - | X X - - | X - - X | - X - -
```

Repeat it three times unchanged. On the fourth repetition, change only the final beat. Notice how a small variation can refresh a repeated idea without destroying its identity.

## Core idea — The Hook Principle

Avoid writing a completely new idea in every bar. The listener needs repetition before variation becomes meaningful.

A useful four-bar model is:

```text
Bar 1 — HOOK
Bar 2 — HOOK (repeat)
Bar 3 — HOOK (small variation)
Bar 4 — PAYOFF
```

The goal is not complexity. The goal is recognition.

## Exercise 1 — Find Your Hook

Using only D5, F5 and G5, write one bar. Loop it for one minute.

Ask: **If someone interrupted me now, could I clap this rhythm from memory?**

If not, simplify it.

## Exercise 2 — Three Versions

Take the hook and create three versions:

- **A — Original:** unchanged.
- **B — Shift:** move one chord attack by one sixteenth note.
- **C — Remove:** delete one attack and replace its duration with the appropriate rest.

Do not change anything else. Compare the three versions and keep the strongest one.

## Exercise 3 — Four-Bar Structure

Develop the chosen hook into four bars:

1. Introduce the hook.
2. Repeat it.
3. Make a small variation.
4. Resolve or provide a payoff.

Repeat/develop this structure to create an **8-bar verse riff**.

## Exercise 4 — Drum Conversation

This time, try writing the drums first.

Start with a simple groove such as:

- Kick: beat 1, the `&` of 2, beat 3
- Snare: beats 2 and 4
- Hi-hat: steady eighth notes

Write the guitar around the kick. Some important guitar attacks can lock to the kick; others can answer it. Avoid making every instrument perform the exact same rhythm unless the unison is intentional.

## Exercise 5 — The Silence Test

Take the riff and remove one complete beat from Bar 4, replacing it with a rest.

Loop Bars 1–5 and listen to the return on Bar 5. If it feels larger, the silence is functioning as part of the arrangement rather than as missing material.

## Listener Test

Do not ask whether the riff is difficult to play. Ask:

**What will the listener remember tomorrow?**

A strong answer might simply be: “that rhythm from Bar 1.”

## Listening Assignment

Choose one metal song you like and ignore tuning, guitar tone and production. Identify:

1. The main rhythmic hook.
2. How many times it repeats.
3. Where it changes.
4. Whether the change is large or small.

Pay attention to how little a memorable riff may actually change.

# Homework

Create **three complete 8-bar riffs**.

## Riff 1 — Industrial Machine

Imagine a relentless factory that never stops.

Requirements:

- One clear rhythmic hook
- Minimal chord movement
- Drums tightly locked to important guitar attacks

## Riff 2 — The Stalker

Imagine something following you and gradually getting closer without attacking.

Use silence and delayed chord changes to create tension.

## Riff 3 — Your Signature

Write the riff that best represents your own writing style so far. Do not deliberately imitate another band.

## Rules

- Tempo: **170 BPM**
- Tuning: **Drop D**
- Allowed chords: **D5, F5, G5**
- Palm mute allowed
- Let Ring allowed
- Accents and heavy accents allowed
- Bass doubles/supports the guitar
- Simple drum groove required

## Producer Challenge

After writing all three riffs, answer:

1. Which single bar is strongest?
2. If you had to delete half of a riff, which half would you keep?
3. Can you clap the hook without touching the guitar?
4. Would you recognize the riff with a clean guitar tone?

If the fourth answer is yes, the riff has an identity beyond distortion and production.

## Riff Vault

Keep the original and at least one revised version of every strong riff. Give each riff a 1–10 rating and add one sentence describing what makes it unique. These ideas will become source material when the course moves from riff writing into complete song arrangement.
